For this project in CSCI 4521, I explored how financial institutions assess creditworthiness using machine learning techniques. In the U.S., financial institutions rely on credit ratings to determine whether to grant loans for mortgages, car purchases, or credit cards. A credit score is a numerical representation of a person's likelihood to repay a loan on time, and higher scores lead to better loan approvals and interest rates. These scores are calculated by credit bureaus using factors such as payment history, outstanding debt, account age, and credit mix. In this analysis, I examined various demographic and financial factors to determine their impact on credit scores and built classification models to predict whether an individual has good or poor credit.

I began by preparing the dataset, loading the provided CSV into a DataFrame, and creating a new column, Rating_Category, to classify individuals as having either Good credit (score of 670 or above) or Poor credit (below 670). Once categorized, I moved on to Data Visualization, selecting four features I believed to be strong indicators of credit ratings. Using Seaborn’s pairplot function, I generated scatter plots colored by credit rating category, allowing me to identify potential correlations between these features and credit scores.

After visualizing the data, I conducted a Data Analysis by interpreting trends in the scatter plots. I identified which features showed the strongest correlation with high credit ratings and provided recommendations for individuals looking to improve their credit score. My findings highlighted specific financial behaviors and demographic factors that tend to align with better credit scores.

Next, I implemented and evaluated Machine Learning Models to classify individuals based on their credit rating. I built two classifiers: one from each of two categories. The first was a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier, where I experimented with both a small and a large value of K to observe the differences in model performance. The second was a baseline classifier, either one that assumed all individuals had good credit or one that assumed none did. After normalizing the dataset and splitting it into training and testing sets, I evaluated the models based on precision, recall, accuracy, and F1-score. I compared how well each model performed on training versus testing data and analyzed why certain classifiers performed better or worse.

Finally, I applied my trained model to a hypothetical credit card applicant. Given their demographic and financial details—such as income, education, and credit card balances—I used KNN analysis to predict whether they would have a good credit rating. I justified my choice of K value, selected key features for the model, and explained my data processing steps. Additionally, I explored what minimal change to the applicant's statistics would be needed to flip the prediction, gaining insight into how small financial decisions impact creditworthiness.

Through this project, I gained hands-on experience applying supervised learning techniques to real-world financial data. By combining data visualization, clustering, classification, and model evaluation, I was able to explore the complex factors that influence credit ratings and build predictive models that mirror real-world financial decision-making.
